# Event Visual Directions

Turn an unclear event brief into three culturally relevant, operationally credible directions whose differences go deeper than color, typography, or reference imagery.

## Scope

Use this V0.1 for youth-culture events, music festivals, nightlife, pop-ups, immersive gatherings, and similar live experiences.

Do not claim that this workflow has been validated for pure corporate-brand or long-term identity briefs. If the request is primarily a brand-identity problem without a live-event experience, explain the boundary and use the method only if the user explicitly wants an adaptation.

This skill develops direction strategy. It does not create final logos, key visuals, production-ready spatial drawings, exact budgets, or a full campaign plan unless the user separately requests those deliverables.

## Establish the Working Brief

Extract what is already known before asking questions:

- event goal and desired audience response;
- core audience and relevant cultural signals;
- venue, format, duration, and expected scale;
- budget level or fixed-budget constraint;
- launch date, promotion runway, and production time;
- existing brand assets and non-negotiable requirements.

Separate facts, client claims, audience hypotheses, and unresolved contradictions. Do not silently turn a vague claim such as “young people like new things” into evidence.

If a missing detail would materially change the concept, ask no more than three focused questions. Prioritize audience motivation, time or promotion runway, and execution constraints. If the user cannot answer, state the assumption and lower the confidence of affected decisions.

## Workflow

### 1. Diagnose the real problem

Distinguish the surface request from the underlying visual-experience problem. Translate demographic labels into a hypothesized desire, tension, identity, emotion, or social behavior.

For example, “young, avant-garde, and interested in nightlife” is not yet a direction. Determine whether the event must deliver belonging, escape, ritual, status, discovery, release, co-creation, or another specific experience. Treat these only as hypotheses until supported.

### 2. Validate current cultural signals

Look for evidence in current audience behavior, relevant social content, recent visual exhibitions or experiences, small-sample conversations, and—when available—the most committed “super players” in the scene.

When the direction depends on what is current or newly popular, use available browsing or search tools. Prefer primary event pages, creator or venue material, and direct audience evidence over trend roundups. Record the source and date. If current research is unavailable, mark the insight as requiring validation rather than inventing proof.

Distinguish:

- **Observed signal:** directly found in current behavior or sources.
- **Interpretation:** what the signal may mean for the audience.
- **Creative leap:** the proposed concept inspired by that interpretation.

Do not present all three as equally certain.

### 3. Find cultural territories

Search for cultural phenomena, rituals, subcultures, stories, or gatherings that share the target audience's desired mechanism. Include local and international territories when useful, but do not assume that an internationally successful case transfers directly.

For each territory, separate its surface from its engine:

- Surface: familiar symbols, palette, costumes, type, motifs, or scenery.
- Engine: why people gather, what they do together, how participation works, what value they share, what transformation occurs, and what remains afterward.

Borrow the engine, not the visual skin. A reference is useful only when its engine explains the audience opportunity and can be translated into an original context.

### 4. Translate the mechanism

Turn the selected cultural engine into an original event premise, not a themed imitation. Show how it becomes:

- a world the audience can understand;
- a reason to participate;
- an interaction, ritual, game, or discovery mechanism;
- a coherent visual grammar;
- an experience suitable for the local cultural context.

When drawing on a culture, identify what is being transformed and avoid presenting a decorative mixture of unrelated cultural symbols as “localization.” Flag cultural sensitivity or authenticity risks instead of hiding them.

### 5. Build three direction skeletons

Develop three directions at the same assumed budget level unless the user specifies otherwise. Each direction needs a distinct proposition before moodboards or stylistic detailing.

Every pair of directions must be substantively different across all six required axes:

1. **Core emotion** — the feeling the experience is designed to produce.
2. **Worldview** — the premise, rules, and meaning of the event world.
3. **Interaction mechanism** — what the audience actually does and how discovery or reward works.
4. **Composition language** — hierarchy, scale, density, rhythm, framing, and image-text relationship.
5. **Material language** — physical or simulated surfaces, tactility, light response, and fabrication character.
6. **Color system** — not merely different hues; change the hierarchy, contrast, saturation, luminosity, spatial use, and relationship to material.

Do not use space behavior or the relationship between people and the event as first-pass differentiation requirements. Derive them after each skeleton passes the six-axis check, then use them to enrich the experience.

### 6. Apply the feasibility gate

Assess each direction against the same real constraints:

- time required for the audience to understand the world;
- promotion and content runway;
- dependency on continuous worldbuilding or education;
- operating staff, performers, NPCs, or specialist collaborators;
- production difficulty and venue constraints;
- number of moving parts and failure points;
- whether the central promise survives simplification.

For a short-run project, favor a premise that can be understood at a glance and enjoyed without learning many rules. If a strong concept requires extended storytelling or operational preparation that the schedule cannot support, do not discard the insight automatically. Extract its smallest testable attraction and propose a reduced experiment, while clearly marking the full version as unsuitable for the current launch.

Do not call a concept feasible merely because it can be drawn convincingly.

### 7. Validate with the right people

Recommend a proportionate validation method:

- quick reactions from people adjacent to the target audience;
- short concept comparisons with representative participants;
- direct conversations with core fans or super players;
- small prototypes of the central interaction;
- a limited-content or limited-area test when the full world is too expensive.

Test the audience promise and participation mechanism, not only whether respondents like the poster.

### 8. Handle direction revisions

When the user asks to revise one existing direction without turning it into a new direction, identify the requested variable and lock the unrequested skeleton axes. For example, a request to make a direction's color system warmer should preserve its core emotion, worldview, interaction mechanism, composition, and material language unless the requested change creates a necessary conflict.

After the revision, check both conditions:

- the direction still expresses its original proposition;
- it has not collapsed into either of the other directions.

If the requested adjustment cannot satisfy both conditions, explain the collision and propose the smallest additional change rather than silently rebuilding the concept.

## Output Contract

Deliver the following in the user's working language and in clear professional terms:

### Brief diagnosis

- surface request;
- underlying visual-experience problem;
- facts, hypotheses, contradictions, and high-impact unknowns;
- audience motivation and supporting evidence with confidence level.

### Cultural opportunity

- relevant cultural territory or territories;
- the mechanism worth transferring;
- surface elements that should not be copied;
- localization, originality, or sensitivity considerations.

### Three directions

For each direction provide:

- name and one-sentence proposition;
- audience promise and core emotion;
- worldview;
- participation or interaction mechanism;
- composition language;
- material language;
- color-system logic;
- optional derived spatial behavior and people-event relationship;
- feasibility conditions, dependencies, risks, and smallest useful test.

### Differentiation matrix

Compare all three directions across the six required axes. Describe the governing logic, not adjective-only differences. Also compare expected audience value, operational dependency, and cognitive load so the client can make a reasoned choice.

### Recommendation

State which direction best balances cultural relevance, distinctiveness, and present constraints. Keep stronger but currently premature concepts visible as future opportunities when appropriate.

## Hard Constraints

- Do not label recolors, font swaps, renamed themes, or alternate reference collages as separate directions.
- Do not copy the recognizable visual skin of a cultural reference when the task is to translate its mechanism.
- Do not use “young people will like it” as sufficient evidence.
- Do not hide dependencies on promotion, content operations, education, performers, or worldbuilding.
- Do not let persuasive visual language override schedule, venue, or execution evidence.
- Do not fabricate trend research, audience interviews, or validation results.
- Keep facts, interpretations, and creative leaps visibly distinct.

## Check, Repair, Recheck

Before delivery, perform a pairwise comparison of all directions.

Reject a direction set when any pair:

- expresses the same core emotion under different names;
- shares the same worldview with cosmetic storytelling changes;
- asks the audience to do essentially the same thing;
- uses the same composition or material logic with a new reference image;
- changes palette while retaining the same color hierarchy and light behavior;
- depends on resources or audience education that the brief cannot support.

When the set fails, identify the earliest shared layer and repair from there:

- shared audience promise: return to the underlying problem;
- shared cultural engine: choose a new cultural territory;
- shared worldview or interaction: rebuild the direction skeleton;
- shared composition, material, or color logic: rebuild the visual grammar;
- feasibility failure: simplify to the smallest testable attraction or mark the direction as future-stage.

Then rerun the six-axis comparison and feasibility gate. Do not repair a structural duplicate by changing only its name, palette, or moodboard.

## Completion Standard

The work is complete only when:

- the real visual-experience problem is stated separately from the surface brief;
- cultural claims are supported, qualified, or explicitly marked for validation;
- three directions pass the six-axis differentiation check;
- each direction explains both visual logic and participation logic;
- feasibility is assessed under comparable constraints;
- the recommendation states its evidence, tradeoffs, and next test.
